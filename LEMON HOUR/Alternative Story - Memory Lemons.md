Title: The Lemon Game

Three friends gather in an old apartment after midnight. The room is warm, dark, and alive with tiny lights. There are candles, plants, old mirrors, and bowls full of lemons on every table, far too many lemons for a normal party.

The main girl arrives late. Her friends are laughing, drinking, cutting lemons, holding them over their eyes like masks. It feels silly, intimate, almost innocent. One of them says, “You have to play. Everybody plays.”

The game is simple: hold two lemon halves over your eyes, smile, and say one thing you wish you could forget.

At first, everyone laughs.

One girl says she wants to forget an ex.
Another says she wants to forget a bad night.
The main girl refuses. She just smiles and says, “I don’t forget things. I collect them.”

The candles flicker.

When she finally presses the lemon halves to her eyes, the room changes. The warm lights stretch into long golden trails. Her friends’ laughter slows down. Through the wet yellow pulp, she sees flashes of places that are not in the apartment: a hallway full of water, a kitchen covered in sunlight, a childhood birthday, a hospital waiting room, a woman crying in a mirror.

She drops the lemons.

Her friends are still smiling.

One friend gently touches her forehead and says, “You saw yours.”

The main girl realizes the lemons are not a joke. They are little windows. Every person who looks through them sees the memory they buried deepest. The girls have been doing this all night, feeding secrets into the room, and the room has been listening.

Then the lemons on the table begin to roll by themselves.

One by one, they fall to the floor and split open, glowing faintly inside. Each lemon releases a tiny fragment of memory: a laugh, a scream, a whispered name, a door closing, a song from years ago.

The apartment fills with ghosts of ordinary pain.

Not horror ghosts — memory ghosts.

The main girl sees herself as a child, hiding under a table while adults argue. She sees her mother younger than she remembers. She sees herself becoming funny, charming, bright — not because life was easy, but because she learned to turn sour things into light.

The friends stop laughing. For the first time, they are all quiet.

The main girl picks up one lemon half from the floor and holds it toward the camera.

She says, softly: “Maybe we don’t forget. Maybe we ferment.”

Then the apartment goes black.

In the final shot, morning light enters the room. The girls are asleep on the floor among hundreds of lemon peels. The main girl wakes up first. She looks calmer. On the table, one fresh lemon remains, untouched.

She smiles.

Cut to black.

Tone: dreamy, dark, feminine, playful-uncanny. Not full horror. More like magical realism with a little psychological bite.

Core idea: lemons become a symbol of memory — sour, bright, painful, cleansing. The film starts like a cute party video and slowly becomes about the things women laugh through because crying would be too obvious.
